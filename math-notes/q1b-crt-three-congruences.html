Problem 1(b)

Chinese Remainder Theorem · the −1 trick, then merge

Three clues this time — but two of them are the same clue in disguise. Spot the hidden −1 and most of the work disappears.
Solve.
x ≡ 1 (mod 3)
x ≡ 4 (mod 5)
x ≡ 6 (mod 7)
Tool: spot disguised −1's, then merge two at a time

Moduli 3, 5, 7 are pairwise coprime, so one answer mod 3 · 5 · 7 = 105.

Spot the disguised −1's

4 ≡ −1 (mod 5) and 6 ≡ −1 (mod 7), so the last two equations are really

x ≡ −1 (mod 5)
x ≡ −1 (mod 7)

Same remainder on both, coprime moduli — so together

x ≡ −1 (mod 35),   i.e.  x ≡ 34 (mod 35)
Why can we merge them into mod 35?

Rewrite each as a divisibility. x ≡ −1 (mod 5) means 5 divides x − (−1) = x + 1. Likewise x ≡ −1 (mod 7) means 7 divides x + 1. So the same number x + 1 is divisible by both 5 and 7.

Use coprimality. Basic fact: if two coprime numbers both divide a value, their product divides it too. Since 5 and 7 share no factor,

5 | (x+1)  and  7 | (x+1)   →   35 | (x+1)   →   x ≡ −1 (mod 35)

Why coprime matters. In general two divisibilities combine to the lcm, not the product. For 5 and 7, lcm = product = 35, so it's clean. But for 4 and 6, lcm(4,6) = 12 — so x ≡ −1 (mod 4) and x ≡ −1 (mod 6) would give mod 12, not mod 24.

And −1 → 34: just a friendlier name for the same clock position, −1 ≡ −1 + 35 = 34 (mod 35).

Merge with the first equation

Now solve

x ≡ 1  (mod 3)
x ≡ 34 (mod 35)

Reduce the big one mod 3: 34 ≡ 1 (mod 3). So both conditions say x ≡ 1 (mod 3) — they already agree. Therefore x ≡ 34 works as-is.

x ≡ 34 (mod 105)  — that is, x = 34 + 105k.

Check: 34 mod 3 = 1 ✓ · 34 mod 5 = 4 ✓ · 34 mod 7 = 6 ✓

Teaching point. Rewriting 4 and 6 as −1 is what makes this fast. Train the eye to see "one less than the modulus = −1." Same trick powers Problem 2(a).
