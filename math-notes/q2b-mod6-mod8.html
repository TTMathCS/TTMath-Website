Problem 2(b)

Splitting the modulus · the non-coprime trap

6 and 8 look harmless — but they share a secret factor. This is the problem that teaches you to look before you split.
Solve.  x ≡ 5 (mod 6)  and  x ≡ 3 (mod 8).
Try it first — show / hide the full solution Tool: split only the coprime part — and watch the trap
The trap: 6 and 8 are not coprime (gcd = 2). You can't CRT them blindly, and you can't split 8 into 2 × 4 either. Handle the shared factor of 2 carefully.

Split the 6 (it factors into coprimes 2 × 3)

x ≡ 5 (mod 6)  ⟺  x ≡ 1 (mod 2)  and  x ≡ 2 (mod 3)

(using 5 ≡ 1 mod 2 and 5 ≡ 2 mod 3.)

Absorb the redundant condition

We also have x ≡ 3 (mod 8), which already forces x odd, i.e. x ≡ 1 (mod 2). So the mod-2 piece is implied — drop it. Left with:

x ≡ 3 (mod 8)
x ≡ 2 (mod 3)

Now 8 and 3 are coprime, so CRT applies cleanly.

Merge

Answer lives mod 8 × 3 = 24. From x ≡ 3 (mod 8), test {3, 11, 19} for x ≡ 2 (mod 3):

x ≡ 11 (mod 24)  — that is, x = 11 + 24k.

Check: 11 mod 6 = 5 ✓ · 11 mod 8 = 3 ✓

The big idea. When moduli overlap, peel them into prime-power pieces (6 = 2·3, 8 = 2³), then reconcile the shared piece — the stronger condition (mod 8) wins over mod 2. If two overlapping conditions contradicted (one even, one odd) there would be no solution — a good "what if?" for the class.
