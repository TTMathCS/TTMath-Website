The Chinese Remainder Theorem

Also called Sun Zi's Theorem (孫子定理). A from-scratch, visual explanation — for teaching Class 6. Open this in any browser.

The whole idea in one sentence: if you know the remainders of a secret number when divided by a few numbers that share no common factor, those remainders pin down exactly one answer — and you can rebuild the secret number from them.

1. First, what is a "remainder" / congruence?

Forget formulas for a second. Think of a clock. A normal clock has 12 hours, so 17 o'clock is the same as 5 o'clock — you go around once (12) and land on 5. We say 17 ≡ 5 (mod 12), read aloud as "17 is congruent to 5, mod 12."

"mod n" just means a clock with n hours. The number after "mod" is the clock size; the answer is where the hand lands. Below is a clock with 7 hours. Type any number of hours and watch where it lands — that landing spot is the remainder.

Clock size (mod): Hours to advance:
17 hours on a 7-hour clock…
17 ≡ 3 (mod 7)
The hand spins all the way around, then stops on the remainder.

So a congruence x ≡ a (mod n) just says: "on an n-hour clock, x lands on a." That's all. The remainder is always one of 0, 1, 2, …, n−1.

2. The puzzle that started it (≈1700 years ago)

In an old Chinese text — Sunzi Suanjing (孫子算經, "The Mathematical Classic of Master Sun"), by Sun Zi (孫子) — a general wants to count his soldiers without counting one by one. He lines them up:

  • in rows of 3 → 2 soldiers left over   (x ≡ 2 mod 3)
  • in rows of 5 → 3 left over   (x ≡ 3 mod 5)
  • in rows of 7 → 2 left over   (x ≡ 2 mod 7)

From just three small leftovers, can we recover the exact count? The Chinese Remainder Theorem says yes — and there is only one answer below 3×5×7 = 105. (It's 23.)

Wait — same Sun Zi as The Art of War? Nope, and it fools a lot of people. The Art of War (孫子兵法) was written by the strategist Sun Wu (孫武) around 500 BCE. This Mathematical Classic (孫子算經) appeared some 700–900 years later (roughly 300–500 CE) from a different "Master Sun" whose real name is lost to history. Same honorific "Sunzi," two completely different people — which is exactly why the theorem is nicknamed 孫子定理, "Sun Zi's Theorem," after the mathematician, not the general.

3. The theorem, stated plainly

Pick clocks of sizes n₁, n₂, …, nₖ that are pairwise coprime — every pair shares no common factor, gcd(nᵢ, nⱼ) = 1 for i ≠ j (e.g. 3, 5, 7). For any chosen landing spots a₁, a₂, …, aₖ, the system

x ≡ a1 (mod n1),   x ≡ a2 (mod n2),   … ,   x ≡ ak (mod nk)

has exactly one solution in the range 0 ≤ x < N — i.e. a single number among 1, 2, …, N — where N is the product of all the clock sizes:

size of the answer range N = n1·n2·⋯·nk = k∏i=1 ni

Every other solution is that one plus a multiple of N.

4. Why it works — see it with your eyes

This is the heart of the lesson. Take two clocks, sizes 3 and 5. Make a grid: rows = remainder on the first clock, columns = remainder on the second clock. Now count x = 0, 1, 2, … and drop each number into the cell (its two remainders). Press play and watch.

Two clock sizes:
Now placing: –
filled once landed twice (collision) never filled
What to notice. With 3 & 5 (coprime), the numbers march diagonally and fill every single cell exactly once — 15 numbers, 15 cells, perfect. That's the theorem: each pair of remainders happens for one and only one x. With 2 & 4 (share a factor of 2), it breaks — some cells get hit twice and others are never reached. That's why "coprime" is non-negotiable.

Said in symbols, the grid is a perfect one-to-one matching: every number below N pairs with exactly one tuple of remainders, and every tuple is used once.

one number ⟷ one tuple of remainders x  ⟷  ( x mod n1,  x mod n2,  … ,  x mod nk )

5. So how do you actually solve one?

Solving a system is just pointing at the right cell and reading the number in it. Choose the remainders you want and the grid will find the unique answer.

I want   x ≡ (mod 3)  and  x ≡ (mod 5)
Pick remainders, then press Find x.

The two hand methods (for the board)

  • Just test. The answer is below N, so try x = 0,1,2,… until both remainders match. Perfect for small N — it's literally walking the grid.
  • The −1 shortcut. If a remainder is one less than its clock (like 4 mod 5, or 6 mod 7), rewrite it as −1. In the class problem x ≡ 4 (mod 5) and x ≡ 6 (mod 7) both become x ≡ −1, so together x ≡ −1 (mod 35) = 34. Fast.

6. The general case — any number of clocks

The grid above shows two clocks, but the theorem works for k clocks at once, exactly as the PDF states it:

x ≡ a₁ (mod n₁),   x ≡ a₂ (mod n₂),   … ,   x ≡ aₖ (mod nₖ)
one unique answer modulo N = k∏i=1 ni  (when the clocks are pairwise coprime).

Build your own system below. Add or remove clocks; the default is the soldier puzzle from Section 2 (three clocks: 3, 5, 7).

Two buttons, two purposes. Show all N checks every number 0 … N−1 and lights the one survivor — this only proves the answer is unique (it really is counting, and you'd never do this by hand). Construct with the formula is the actual method: it builds the answer in one shot with

CRT construction formula x ≡ k∑i=1 ai Mi yi  (mod N),    Mi = N / ni,   yi ≡ Mi−1 (mod ni)

ai = the target remainder  ·  Mi = the selector (0 on every other clock)  ·  yi = the inverse that tunes it to land on 1

The idea: term i is divisible by every other clock's modulus, so it contributes ai on clock i and 0 on all the others — each clock gets set independently, no searching.

Worked example 1 — the soldier puzzle by formula

x ≡ 2 (mod 3),   x ≡ 3 (mod 5),   x ≡ 2 (mod 7),   so  N = 3·5·7 = 105.

ni ai Mi = N/ni Mi mod ni
(reduce first)		 yi = Mi−1 mod ni aiMiyi
323522  (2·2 ≡ 1)2·35·2 = 140
532111  (1·1 ≡ 1)3·21·1 = 63
721511  (1·1 ≡ 1)2·15·1 = 30

Reduce Mi mod ni first, then invert that small number — Mi−1 = (Mi mod ni)−1. Here rows 2 and 3 reduce to 1, whose inverse is just 1.

x ≡ 140 + 63 + 30 = 233 ≡ 23  (mod 105)

Check: 23 mod 3 = 2 ✓ · 23 mod 5 = 3 ✓ · 23 mod 7 = 2 ✓ — the same 23 the soldier count gives.

Worked example 2 — where the inverse really does work

Solve x ≡ 2 (mod 5) and x ≡ 3 (mod 7),   N = 5·7 = 35. Here the inverses are not just 1, so you can see the step.

  • M1 = 35/5 = 7;   7 mod 5 = 2, so we need 2·y1 ≡ 1 (mod 5) → y1 = 3  (2·3 = 6 ≡ 1). Term = 2·7·3 = 42.
  • M2 = 35/7 = 5;   5 mod 7 = 5, so we need 5·y2 ≡ 1 (mod 7) → y2 = 3  (5·3 = 15 ≡ 1). Term = 3·5·3 = 45.
x ≡ 42 + 45 = 87 ≡ 17  (mod 35)

Check: 17 mod 5 = 2 ✓ · 17 mod 7 = 3 ✓.   Notice yi is the inverse of Mi (not of ni).

Two things to point out. (1) Add a fourth coprime clock (say mod 11) and N just grows — still exactly one answer. (2) Add a clock that shares a factor with another (e.g. another "mod 6" next to "mod 3") and the guarantee breaks: you'll get either no answer or several. That's the pairwise-coprime condition doing its job.

7. Why anyone cares today

All three of these are the exact same move as the soldier puzzle: work with small remainders, then glue them into one answer with CRT.

Your phone, every secure website (RSA)

In one line: CRT doesn't create or crack the private key — it just makes the key owner's own decrypt / sign step about 4× faster. Same answer, less time.

Who knows what. The owner generated the key, so they alone know the two secret primes p and q. Everyone else sees only the public modulus n = p · q and the public exponent. Factoring n back into p · q is infeasible — that's the entire security of RSA — so only the owner can take the shortcut below.

The slow way. To read a ciphertext c, the owner must compute m = cd mod n with the private exponent d. Both d and n are ~2048-bit numbers, so this single power is expensive.

What the owner actually does (the CRT way), using their private p and q:

  1. shrink the exponent: dp = d mod (p−1) and dq = d mod (q−1);
  2. do two small powers — c raised to dp mod p, and c raised to dq mod q — each on half-size numbers, giving mp and mq;
  3. glue with CRT: solve x ≡ mp (mod p), x ≡ mq (mod q) — its one answer mod n is the message m.

So what is CRT's job here? Steps 1–2 only produce two partial views of the answer — the message seen on the p-clock and on the q-clock. Those halves are not the message. CRT is the step that fuses the two halves back into the single number m. Without it you'd be stuck holding two pieces. And it pays off because doubling a modulus's bit-length makes a power ~8× costlier (cubic cost): one 2048-bit power ≈ 8 units of work, while two 1024-bit powers ≈ 2 units — roughly ¼ the time, the speed-up baked into every TLS library.

Tiny example with toy primes. Let p = 5, q = 11, so n = 55, and suppose the private exponent is d = 27. To decrypt c = 17: 
d_p = 27 mod (5−1)  = 3   →   m_p = 17³ mod 5  = 2³ mod 5  = 3
d_q = 27 mod (11−1) = 7   →   m_q = 17⁷ mod 11 = 6⁷ mod 11 = 8

glue:  x ≡ 3 (mod 5),  x ≡ 8 (mod 11)   →   x = 8
The recovered message is m = 8 — found using p and q, never touching the big mod-55 power. (Finding d itself, and the inverses inside the glue step, use the Euclidean algorithm.)

The soldier trick, modernized (residue number systems)

In plain terms: CRT lets a computer treat one huge number as a bundle of small independent numbers — so it can crunch them faster and in parallel, then rebuild the real answer at the end.

Fix a set of coprime moduli m₁, m₂, …, mₖ (small enough to fit a CPU register). Store a big number X not as digits but as its remainder tuple (X mod m₁, …, X mod mₖ). By CRT that tuple is X — exactly one big number gives it, as long as X < N = m₁·⋯·mₖ. (That's the grid filling once per cell.)

How CRT is used: to add or multiply two giant numbers, work on each remainder independently — (X+Y) mod mᵢ = (X mod mᵢ + Y mod mᵢ) mod mᵢ — with no carries linking the lanes, so every lane runs in parallel on separate hardware. CRT reassembles the true answer from the tuple at the end. Used in signal-processing and crypto chips where raw multiply speed matters.

Secret sharing (splitting a key safely)

In plain terms: CRT is the rule that decides exactly how many pieces count as "enough" — gather the threshold and the secret is uniquely pinned down; gather fewer and it stays genuinely hidden.

You want to split a secret S among several people so that any t of them together can recover it, but t−1 learn nothing. One classic scheme (Mignotte / Asmuth–Bloom) hands person i the share sᵢ = S mod mᵢ for chosen coprime moduli mᵢ.

How CRT is used: collect enough shares and the product of their moduli exceeds the range S lives in, so CRT pins down one value — the secret. Collect too few and that product is too small; many different S values fit the same remainders, so the shares reveal nothing. The whole security threshold is just "do the gathered moduli multiply to something big enough to make the CRT answer unique?"

Real example: HashiCorp Vault's "unseal" runs on exactly this k-of-n idea — it splits the master key into, say, 5 shares and needs any 3 to unlock, so no single admin can open it alone. Vault uses Shamir's Secret Sharing (built from polynomials, not CRT), but the goal is identical; CRT is simply a different way to build the same threshold guarantee.
