Fermat's Little Theorem

The tool that crushes giant exponents down to tiny ones

The theorem. If p is prime and p ∤ a (p does not divide a), then
ap−1 ≡ 1  (mod p)

1. What it says, in plain words

Pick a prime clock size p. Take any number a that isn't a multiple of p. Keep multiplying a by itself — a, a², a³, … — and read each result on the p-clock. The theorem promises that by the time you reach the (p−1)-th power, you land back exactly on 1.

Examples we use in class:

mod 7 :  a⁶ ≡ 1   (for any a not divisible by 7)
mod 11:  a¹⁰ ≡ 1  (for any a not divisible by 11)
mod 5 :  a⁴ ≡ 1

Quick check mod 7 with a = 3:  3⁶ = 729 = 104·7 + 1 ≡ 1 (mod 7). ✓

2. Why it matters: shrink the exponent

This is the whole reason it appears in Problem 2(c). A power like x¹²¹ looks terrifying, but mod a prime only the exponent matters, and only mod (p−1). Write the exponent as a multiple of p−1 plus a small leftover:

mod 7 (so p−1 = 6):   121 = 6·20 + 1
x¹²¹ = (x⁶)²⁰ · x¹ ≡ 1²⁰ · x = x   (mod 7)

That rewrite uses two plain algebra rules — xᵃ⁺ᵇ = xᵃ·xᵇ and (xᵃ)ᵇ = xᵃᵇ — to pull out chunks of x⁶, each of which Fermat turns into 1. When the exponent divides evenly there's no leftover and the whole thing is 1:

36 = 6·6 + 0   →   x³⁶ = (x⁶)⁶ ≡ 1⁶ = 1   (mod 7)

Picture x⁶ ≡ 1 as a "reset": the quotient counts full resets (all become 1), the remainder is the surviving exponent. A monster exponent collapses to its remainder. The rule to write on the board: mod a prime p, reduce the exponent mod (p−1) first.

3. The one condition: p must not divide a

If p divides a, then a ≡ 0, and every power is 0 — never 1. So the theorem needs p ∤ a. That's why in Problem 2(c) we first rule out the case 7 | x (it gives 2 ≡ 0, impossible) before applying the theorem. Skipping that check loses marks.

4. Why it's true (a short proof)

Multiplying by a just shuffles the clock

List the nonzero residues 1, 2, …, p−1. Multiply each by a (mod p):

a·1,  a·2,  …,  a·(p−1)   (mod p)

These are the same p−1 numbers, just reordered. (No two can be equal: if a·i ≡ a·j, cancel a — allowed since p ∤ a — to get i ≡ j. And none is 0.)

Multiply them all together

The product of the shuffled list equals the product of the original list:

p−1∏i=1(a·i)  ≡  p−1∏i=1i  (mod p)

Pull the p−1 copies of a out of the left product — that is ap−1 — and both sides carry the same factorial (p−1)! = 1·2·⋯·(p−1):

ap−1 · (p−1)! ≡ (p−1)!  (mod p)

Cancel (p−1)!

None of 1, …, p−1 is divisible by the prime p, so (p−1)! can be cancelled from both sides:

a^(p−1) ≡ 1 (mod p).    ∎
