Problem 4

Order of an element · the period of the powers

How many times do you double before you're back to the start? Place a bet on the number, then let's hunt the cycle.
Find the smallest positive n with 2ⁿ ≡ 1 (mod 35).

The word order has a precise meaning here — and it's exactly what this problem asks for.

The order of a (mod n), written ordn(a), is the smallest positive power that brings a back to 1: the smallest d with aᵈ ≡ 1 (mod n).

Equivalently it's the length of the cycle of powers. List a, a², a³, … on the n-clock; they eventually loop, and the number of steps before you return to 1 is the order.

So "find the smallest n with 2ⁿ ≡ 1 (mod 35)" is literally "find the order of 2 mod 35." The demo lower down shows the cycle returning to 1.

Why the name "order"?

The powers you can reach — {a, a², …, aᵈ = 1} — form a small set that cycles forever. The order is the size of that set: how many distinct values are in the loop. "Order" is just the classic word for "how many things are in this group," so the order of the element = the length of its cycle. For 2 mod 7 the cycle is 2, 4, 1 — three values — so the order is 3.

Try it first — show / hide the full solution Tool: order = lcm of the orders mod each prime factor

35 = 5 × 7, and gcd(2, 5) = gcd(2, 7) = 1, so the order of 2 mod 35 is the lcm of its order mod 5 and mod 7.

Why is the order mod 35 the lcm of the orders mod 5 and mod 7?

Step 1 — split. Since 5 and 7 are coprime, one condition becomes two:

2ⁿ ≡ 1 (mod 35)   ⟺   2ⁿ ≡ 1 (mod 5)  AND  2ⁿ ≡ 1 (mod 7)

(a power lands on 1 on the 35-clock exactly when it lands on 1 on both small clocks at once.)

Step 2 — each clock forces a divisibility. Write d₅, d₇ for the orders of 2 mod 5 and mod 7. A power equals 1 exactly when the exponent is a multiple of that order:

2ⁿ ≡ 1 (mod 5)  ⟺  d₅ | n          2ⁿ ≡ 1 (mod 7)  ⟺  d₇ | n

(that's the "n = qd + r" argument shown in Step 1 below.)

Step 3 — both at once = lcm. So we need d₅ | n and d₇ | n: n is a common multiple of both. A number divisible by both is exactly a multiple of their least common multiple:

2ⁿ ≡ 1 (mod 35)  ⟺  lcm(d₅, d₇) | n

The smallest positive such n is lcm(d₅, d₇) itself — and the smallest n is exactly what "order" means. Hence order mod 35 = lcm(d₅, d₇). Here that's lcm(4, 3) = 12.

Order of 2 mod 5

2¹ = 2,  2² = 4,  2³ ≡ 3,  2⁴ ≡ 1     →   order 4

So 2ⁿ ≡ 1 (mod 5) happens exactly when 4 divides n.

Why does hitting 1 mean "4 divides n"?

We list the powers until the first return to 1: that first time is 2⁴ ≡ 1, so the order is 4.

Now take any n and divide it by 4: n = 4q + r with 0 ≤ r < 4. Then

2ⁿ = (2⁴)^q · 2ʳ ≡ 1 · 2ʳ = 2ʳ   (mod 5)

For 2ⁿ ≡ 1 we'd need 2ʳ ≡ 1 with r smaller than 4 — but 4 is the smallest exponent that returns to 1, so the only possibility is r = 0. That means 4 | n. (The same argument in Step 2 gives 3 | n.)

Order of 2 mod 7

2¹ = 2,  2² = 4,  2³ = 8 ≡ 1           →   order 3

So 2ⁿ ≡ 1 (mod 7) happens exactly when 3 divides n (same argument as Step 1).

Combine

ord35(2) = lcm( ord5(2), ord7(2) ) = lcm(4, 3) = 12

We need 4 | n (Step 1) and 3 | n (Step 2) at once, so n is a common multiple of 4 and 3 — the smallest is their lcm.

Why the least common multiple?

A valid n must be divisible by both 4 and 3, so it's a common multiple of them: 12, 24, 36, … The smallest positive one is the least common multiple, lcm(4, 3) = 12.

Since gcd(4, 3) = 1 (no shared factor), the lcm equals the product 4 × 3 = 12. So the smallest n is 12.

(If the two orders had shared a factor, the lcm would be smaller than the product — e.g. lcm(4, 6) = 12, not 24.)

smallest n = 12.

Check: 2¹² = 4096 = 35·117 + 1, so 2¹² ≡ 1 (mod 35). ✓   No smaller n works, since any working n must be a multiple of both 4 and 3.

The honest remark from the handout. There is no neat formula for the order in general — you find one working exponent (Fermat hands you p−1) and test its divisors. The lcm-of-prime-orders shortcut above is the best general tool, and that's as good as it gets.
