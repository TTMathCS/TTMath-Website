Problem 3

Fermat's Little Theorem · a short proof

A proof so short it feels like a card trick — two lines of Fermat and you're done.
Prove. If gcd(a, 77) = 1 then a³⁰ ≡ 1 (mod 77).
Try it yourself first — show the full solutionHide the solution Tool: Fermat on each prime factor, then bump to a common exponent

Coprime to 77 means coprime to 7 and to 11

Since 77 = 7 × 11, gcd(a, 77) = 1 gives gcd(a, 7) = 1 and gcd(a, 11) = 1. Fermat:

a⁶ ≡ 1  (mod 7)
a¹⁰ ≡ 1 (mod 11)

Raise both to reach exponent 30

30 is a multiple of both 6 and 10  (30 = 6·5 = 10·3):

a³⁰ = (a⁶)⁵  ≡ 1⁵ = 1   (mod 7)
a³⁰ = (a¹⁰)³ ≡ 1³ = 1   (mod 11)

Combine with coprimality

So 7 and 11 both divide a³⁰ − 1. Since they are coprime, their product 77 divides it too:

7 | (a³⁰ − 1)  and  11 | (a³⁰ − 1)  ⟹  77 | (a³⁰ − 1)
a³⁰ ≡ 1 (mod 77).  ∎
Teaching point. 30 is exactly lcm(6, 10) — the smallest exponent that works for both primes at once. That's the bridge straight into Problem 4 (order = lcm of orders).

Your turn

Prove a¹² ≡ 1 (mod 65) whenever gcd(a, 65) = 1.

Show answer

65 = 5 · 13. By Fermat a⁴ ≡ 1 (mod 5) so a¹² = (a⁴)³ ≡ 1; and a¹² ≡ 1 (mod 13) directly (12 = 13 − 1). Both 5 and 13 divide a¹² − 1, so a¹² ≡ 1 (mod 65).
